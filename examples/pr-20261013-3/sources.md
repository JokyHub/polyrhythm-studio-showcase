# Sources — pr-20261013-3

Subject: Orchestre Poly-Rythmo de Cotonou (Cotonou, Benin — called Dahomey until 1975), with focus on The 1st Album (recorded 1973; Analog Africa reissue 2011). Genre: afrobeat. Pillar: Scenes, lineage, and context. Format: thread of 3–5 posts.

Facts in thread order (who, where, when, what changed, what to hear):

1. Who/where/when: Bandleader Mélomé Clément formed the group in Cotonou in 1968, first as Orchestre Poly-Disco; the "Tout Puissant" (T.P.) prefix is sometimes used. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orchestre_Poly-Rythmo_de_Cotonou (retrieved 2026-10-12)
   - 1968 as the start year is confirmed independently by World Music Network's obituary ("started their career in 1968") — https://worldmusic.net/blogs/news/obituary-melome-clement (retrieved 2026-10-12) — and by Pan African Music's 2016 interview, which names 1968 and a 50th anniversary in 2018 — https://pan-african-music.com/en/lorchestre-poly-rythmo-from-the-streets-to-the-worldwide-groove/ (retrieved 2026-10-12). Other dates are listed under Conflicts.
2. Who: Two independent sources describe Clément as the band's saxophonist and leader. World Music Network calls him "conductor and saxophonist" — https://worldmusic.net/blogs/news/obituary-melome-clement (retrieved 2026-10-12). PopMatters (Deanne Sole, 18 July 2013) calls him "saxophonist and Orchestre-founder" — https://www.popmatters.com/171902-orchestre-poly-rythmo-the-skeletal-essence-of-voodoo-funk-1960-1980-2495753634.html (retrieved 2026-10-12)
3. The sound: The Austin Chronicle (Austin Powell, 18 Dec 2009) lists the band's ingredients as James Brown's soul, Nigerian highlife, Afro-Cuban jazz, Fela Kuti's Afrobeat and Vodoun ritual rhythms. — https://www.austinchronicle.com/music/2009-12-18/929985/ (retrieved 2026-10-12)
   - The Quietus (Andy Thomas, 18 July 2011) also points to sacred vodun rhythms rooted in Dahomey, alongside Fela, James Brown and psych-rock organ — https://thequietus.com/articles/06603-orchestre-poly-rythmo-de-cotonou-the-first-album-review (retrieved 2026-10-12)
4. The work: The 1st Album was recorded in Lagos, Nigeria, for Adissa Seidou's Albarika Store label in Cotonou. The band went to Lagos for its better studios. — https://worldmusic.co.uk/orchestre_poly-rythmo_the_1st_album_1973 (retrieved 2026-10-12)
   - The Quietus names the studio as EMI in Lagos — https://thequietus.com/articles/06603-orchestre-poly-rythmo-de-cotonou-the-first-album-review (retrieved 2026-10-12)
   - Rush Hour's catalogue gives "recorded in Lagos in 1973 for the Albarika Store label" — https://www.rushhour.nl/node/109405 (retrieved 2026-10-12)
5. Who: Before the sessions the band poached singer Vincent Ahéhéhinnou from the rival band Daho Jazz. He composed all four tracks of the album. — https://worldmusic.co.uk/orchestre_poly-rythmo_the_1st_album_1973 (retrieved 2026-10-12)
   - The Quietus also says Ahéhéhinnou wrote all four compositions — https://thequietus.com/articles/06603-orchestre-poly-rythmo-de-cotonou-the-first-album-review (retrieved 2026-10-12)
6. The session story: The album was recorded twice. Albarika rejected the first session because of too much background noise, probably from the organ's amplification. — https://worldmusic.co.uk/orchestre_poly-rythmo_the_1st_album_1973 (retrieved 2026-10-12)
   - The 2011 Analog Africa reissue (Limited Dance Edition No. 1, CD cat. AACDDE01) has two tracks from each session. The two from the rejected session were released there for the first time. Tracklist: "Ou C'est Lui Ou C'est Moi", "Ye Ye We Nou Mi", "La La La La", "Egni Miton? Nin Mi Na Wa Gbin" — https://www.rushhour.nl/node/109405 (retrieved 2026-10-12). The UK release date was Monday 13 June 2011 — https://worldmusic.co.uk/orchestre_poly-rythmo_the_1st_album_1973 (retrieved 2026-10-12)
7. Size of the catalogue (attributed): The "about 500 tracks" figure comes from the label side. PopMatters reports that publicists said Samy Ben Redjeb (Analog Africa) had gathered "a collection of about 500 T.P. Orchestre Poly-Rythmo de Cotonou tracks", mostly recorded 1969–1983 — https://www.popmatters.com/171902-orchestre-poly-rythmo-the-skeletal-essence-of-voodoo-funk-1960-1980-2495753634.html (retrieved 2026-10-12). Pan African Music's 2016 interview gives "more than 500 records" — https://pan-african-music.com/en/lorchestre-poly-rythmo-from-the-streets-to-the-worldwide-groove/ (retrieved 2026-10-12). The Austin Chronicle gives "more than 50 LPs and roughly twice as many singles" before the band split in 1982 — https://www.austinchronicle.com/music/2009-12-18/929985/ (retrieved 2026-10-12). Use "hundreds of recordings", or attribute "about 500 tracks" to the Analog Africa archive.
8. What changed (reissues): Soundway released The Kings of Benin: Urban Groove 1972–80 in 2004. Analog Africa followed with anthologies starting with Vol. 1, The Vodoun Effect (2008), and Vol. 2, Echos Hypnotiques. — https://www.austinchronicle.com/music/2009-12-18/929985/ (retrieved 2026-10-12)
   - Songlines also credits a 2004 Soundway compilation and then two Analog Africa collections for the band's reputation in the West — https://songlines.co.uk/review/cotonou-club (via search result 2026-10-12; page not opened)
9. What changed (revival): A French journalist found the band in 2007. It played its first European concert in 2009 ("last year", in an article of 9 Dec 2010). — https://ra.co/news.aspx?id=13252 (retrieved 2026-10-12)
   - Splinters & Candy (9 July 2012) says the band "didn't play outside of Africa until 2009" — https://splintersandcandy.com/orchestre-poly-rythmo-cotonou-club/ (retrieved 2026-10-12)
10. Cotonou Club: Strut released it in March 2011 (RA gives 19 March 2011). It was recorded in Paris and produced by Elodie Maillot. Guests are Angélique Kidjo on "Gbeti Madjro", Fatoumata Diawara on "Mariage / C'est Moi Ou C'est Lui", and Paul Thomson and Nick McCarthy of Franz Ferdinand on "Lion Is Burning". — https://ra.co/news.aspx?id=13252 ; https://worldmusiccentral.org/?p=9255 ; https://splintersandcandy.com/orchestre-poly-rythmo-cotonou-club/ ; tracklist and guest credits from https://orchestrepoly-rythmo.bandcamp.com/album/cotonou-club (all retrieved 2026-10-12)
11. Clément's death: Mélomé Clément died of a heart attack on 17 December 2012, aged 67. His last concerts were that summer in France and Finland. — https://worldmusic.net/blogs/news/obituary-melome-clement (post dated 2 Jan 2013; retrieved 2026-10-12)
   - PopMatters (July 2013) independently says he died "last December, at the age of 67" — https://www.popmatters.com/171902-orchestre-poly-rythmo-the-skeletal-essence-of-voodoo-funk-1960-1980-2495753634.html (retrieved 2026-10-12)
12. ## Conflicts / not verified
- **Founding date:** 1968 is supported by World Music Network, Pan African Music (2016 interview) and Wikipedia. Other dates in circulation: the Austin Chronicle says it began as a trio in 1964 and joined the Sunny Blacks Band about four years later; Pan African Music's own 2016 release article says 1966 as Orchestre Poly-Disco; PopMatters (per search snippet) says 1968 or 1969. Recommended wording: "founded in Cotonou in 1968 (some accounts trace a trio back to 1964)", or simply "late 1960s".
- **Clément's instrument:** Wikipedia calls him a singer and guitarist. World Music Network, PopMatters and the Austin Chronicle call him a saxophonist; Jabulani Radio (search snippet only) says saxophone, accordion and guitar. Recommended wording: "bandleader Mélomé Clément", with no instrument named, or "saxophonist and bandleader" (two independent sources).
- **"~500 songs":** No primary count exists. Attribute it as above, or say "hundreds of recordings".
- **The 1st Album, original catalogue number:** "Albarika Store ALS 005" appeared only in a search-result summary. It is not confirmed on any page I opened, so do not use it. The label (Albarika Store) and the year of recording (1973) are confirmed.
- **The 1st Album, track spelling:** Rush Hour gives "Ye Ye We Nou Mi"; worldmusic.co.uk gives "Weye We Nou Mi". Avoid naming that track.
- **Studio:** The Quietus says EMI studios in Lagos; the other sources say only "Lagos". Safe wording: "recorded in Lagos".
- **Album date:** One search result mentioned a Mojo review dating it to 1974. That page was not opened and probably refers to a different release. 1973 is used by Analog Africa's own title, The 1st Album (1973), and by every review I opened.
- **Franz Ferdinand guest:** Spelled "Thomson" on the official Bandcamp page and "Thompson" in Splinters & Candy. Use Bandcamp's "Paul Thomson".
- **Producer of Cotonou Club:** Elodie Maillot is named in Splinters & Candy and the World Music Central press piece ("produced by Maillot"). RA does not name the producer.
- **Wikipedia's 2009 line-up:** It says the band reformed as a 10-piece with five original members (Clément, Ahéhéhinnou, Maximus Ajanohun, Pierre Loko, Gustave Bentho). Only Wikipedia states this; the "ten-piece" detail appears in a search snippet only. Do not use without a second source.
- **Wikipedia's 1982 deaths:** It says Papillon and Yehouessi Léopold died in 1982. Not cross-checked; do not use.
- Diacritics: Mélomé Clément; Vincent Ahéhéhinnou (press often writes "Ahehehinnou"); Angélique Kidjo; Cotonou; Bénin/Benin (English: Benin); Dahomey was the country's name in 1973.

## Listening angle (opinion)
- "Ou C'est Lui Ou C'est Moi", the opener of The 1st Album. To my ear the thing to follow is the drums against the organ. Yehouessi Léopold's kit keeps a rolling, cyclical pattern while the organ stabs and drones over it, and Ahéhéhinnou's vocal rides on top in call and response with the band. Listen to how little the groove changes, and how the tension builds through repetition rather than chord changes. (The Quietus compares Léopold to Tony Allen; that is their comparison, not ours.)
- Alternative, if the link below is used: "Gbeti Madjro" (feat. Angélique Kidjo) on Cotonou Club, a remake of one of the band's classics with the same tight horn-and-guitar interlock, recorded nearly four decades later.
