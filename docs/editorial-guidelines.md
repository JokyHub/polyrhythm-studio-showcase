# WeLoveJazz editorial guidelines

**Status:** approved editorial baseline (BD-39)

**Applies to:** all public-facing WeLoveJazz copy and its accompanying captions, alt text, and links.
**Primary channel:** X; the standards also apply when a draft is adapted for another channel.

## 1. Editorial purpose and decision rule

WeLoveJazz helps curious listeners discover jazz and jazz-adjacent music with context they can trust. A post should give the reader a reason to listen now, while making it easy to distinguish a verified fact from the editor's interpretation.

Publish only when the draft does all of the following:

1. Names a specific, accurately identified artist, release, track, performance, or scene.
2. Offers one useful listening angle rather than a pile of descriptors.
3. Supports every factual claim with retained editorial evidence, or frames it clearly as opinion.
4. Gives the reader a practical next action, normally a listening link or a concise prompt to seek out the work.
5. Meets the current platform-format and approval requirements in the product requirements document.

When discovery material is incomplete, uncertain, or conflicting, ask for clarification or hold the item. Do not fill gaps with plausible-sounding copy.

## 2. Tone of voice

### Core voice

Write as an informed, generous jazz listener sharing a considered recommendation with another curious listener. The voice is **specific, warm, concise, and grounded**. It welcomes both established listeners and people finding their way into the music without talking down to either group.

| Do | Do not |
| --- | --- |
| Lead with a concrete musical detail, context, or listening invitation. | Lead with empty superlatives or a generic claim that something is “good.” |
| Use plain language and explain specialist terms when they are essential. | Gatekeep with unexplained jargon, scene credentials, or tests of taste. |
| Make a confident recommendation when the evidence supports it. | State interpretation, speculation, or taste as objective fact. |
| Credit artists, collaborators, labels, photographers, and sources where relevant. | Erase collaborators, imply ownership of others’ work, or make unsupported credit claims. |
| Be conversational and economical. | Sound like advertising copy, a press release, an academic abstract, or an automated content feed. |

### Writing mechanics

- Prefer active voice, concrete nouns, and short sentences. Name the artist and work early.
- Describe what can be heard: rhythm, texture, ensemble interaction, mood, arrangement, or recording context. Avoid stacking vague adjectives.
- Use first person sparingly for a clearly labelled reaction (for example, “I keep returning to…”). Do not imply a collective consensus with “everyone knows” or “we all agree.”
- Use sentence case, standard punctuation, and accessible language. Preserve artist-preferred spelling and diacritics when known.
- Use emojis only when they add meaning and never as a substitute for description. Avoid more than one in a post.
- Address sensitive histories, identities, and cultural traditions with care and specificity. Do not exoticize artists, scenes, places, or instruments.

## 3. Content pillars

Every item must have one primary pillar. A secondary pillar is optional only when it makes the post clearer.

| Pillar | Editorial job | Required ingredients | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **New and recent listening** | Surface a newly released or newly encountered work worth hearing. | Artist, work, release context/date when asserted, one audible reason to listen, and a listening route. | Treating “new” as a fact without a verified release context. |
| **Deep cuts and rediscoveries** | Return attention to overlooked, archival, or catalogue music. | Precise work/artist identification, a verified historical or catalogue detail if included, and why it rewards attention now. | Calling something forgotten, obscure, or influential without support. |
| **Musician and collaborator focus** | Highlight a player, composer, arranger, producer, label, or collective and their contribution. | Named contribution, the relevant work, and credit supported by a reliable source. | Reducing collaborative work to a single star or claiming roles from inference. |
| **Listening notes** | Help a reader hear a specific musical moment or relationship. | A clearly framed editorial observation tied to a track, passage, performance, or recording. | Presenting subjective listening notes as technical fact. |
| **Scenes, lineage, and context** | Connect music to a place, community, movement, or influence with care. | Specific context, credible evidence for factual context, and a direct connection to the featured music. | Stereotypes, tourism language, essentialism, or sweeping genre histories. |
| **Editorial selections** | Curate a small, purposeful set around a listening question. | Clear selection rule, complete credits/links, and one sentence explaining the connection. | Listicles assembled around trends, popularity, or quantity alone. |

## 4. Supported content formats

Draft to the approved format below; all formats remain subject to current X limits and the human review/approval workflow. Links and rights-cleared media must be validated before publication.

| Format | Use when | Structure | Minimum editorial checks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Single recommendation** | One track, release, performance, or artist has a clear listening hook. | Hook → artist and title → one supported fact or labelled observation → listening link/invitation. | Correct artist/title; factual claims sourced; link resolves. |
| **Listening note** | The value is in a close, subjective observation. | Identify the exact work → describe what to listen for → invite the reader to hear it. | The observation is recognizably opinion; no unverified technical claims. |
| **Context post** | Verified context changes how a work can be heard. | Specific context → connection to the work → listening invitation. | Context claims have sources; the connection is not overstated. |
| **Credit spotlight** | A contributor’s role merits focused recognition. | Name contributor and role → name the work → explain the contribution → link. | Role and work credits are verified. |
| **Short selection / thread** | Several items answer one focused listening question. | Opening premise → one item per post with credit and reason → closing listening route. | Each item stands alone, the selection criterion is explicit, and every post passes the same evidence checks. |
| **Visual-led post** | An approved image, cover, or performance still adds real editorial value. | Descriptive caption with artist/work/context → rights/credit-aware asset → meaningful alt text. | Asset provenance and credit recorded; alt text describes the image rather than repeating the caption. |

Do not use polls, engagement bait, reactive commentary, memes, quote-post dunking, or automated reply threads as editorial formats. They may be considered only after a documented policy change and explicit human approval.

## 5. Accuracy, attribution, and links

- Treat names, release dates, personnel, labels, locations, awards, quotations, samples, genre claims, and “first/only/most” statements as factual claims requiring evidence.
- Attribute a claim in copy when its source or perspective matters. Retain the supporting source URL and retrieval time in the review package even when the source is not linked publicly.
- Link to an official artist, label, venue, release, or other approved destination whenever practical. Never present an affiliate, tracking, or unverified upload link as an official listening link.
- Quote only when the wording is material, the source is identifiable, and use is appropriate. Otherwise paraphrase accurately and attribute the idea.
- Credit image makers and rights holders according to the asset record. If provenance or permission is unresolved, do not use the asset.
- If a fact cannot be verified, remove it, recast it as an explicitly personal listening response, or hold the draft for research.

## 6. Banned patterns

The following patterns fail editorial QA and must be removed or rewritten:

1. **Fabrication or certainty laundering:** invented details, unsourced biographical claims, or wording that makes an uncertain match sound confirmed.
2. **Empty hype:** “must-hear,” “legendary,” “genius,” “the future of jazz,” “best ever,” or similar superlatives without precise, supported substance.
3. **Engagement bait:** requests for likes, reposts, follows, tags, or replies that do not serve a genuine editorial question; rage bait and manufactured disagreement. A single sincere,
   specific listening question at the end of a post is allowed (owner decision, 2026-10-16).
4. **Gatekeeping and elitism:** dismissing listeners, genres, artists, or scenes; implying that enjoyment requires insider status; jargon used to exclude.
5. **Stereotyping or exoticizing:** reducing artists or music to nationality, race, gender, geography, or a vague idea of “authenticity.”
6. **Unlicensed or misleading media use:** posting assets without recorded provenance/permission, omitting required credit, or implying an artist endorsement.
7. **Promotional disguise:** undisclosed paid, affiliate, gifted, or partner material presented as an independent recommendation.
8. **Template spam:** interchangeable copy, repetitive openings, excessive hashtags, keyword stuffing, or emoji strings.
9. **Unsupported comparisons and rankings:** claims that an artist is the next, only, greatest, or definitive version of another artist or style without a precise, supportable editorial reason.
10. **Privacy and harm:** publishing private contact details, unverified allegations, sensitive personal information, or content that could reasonably endanger a person or community.

## 7. Examples

Examples below show the standard of application. Bracketed text is a production placeholder: replace it only after the associated fact, link, and credit are verified.

### A. Specific recommendation

**Compliant**

> The first minute of **[Track]** by **[Artist]** makes space before it makes a statement: **[specific audible detail]**. Released on **[Label/Release]**, it is a strong entry point into **[accurate, sourced context]**. Listen: **[approved link]**.

Why it works: it identifies the work, gives the reader a concrete listening angle, separates audible observation from sourced context, and provides a validated route to listen.

**Non-compliant**

> This is the future of jazz. **[Artist]** is an absolute genius—stop what you’re doing and stream this now!!! 🔥🔥🔥 #jazz #music #viral

Why it fails: it relies on hype, makes an unsupported ranking claim, offers no usable context, and uses engagement-oriented styling instead of editorial value.

### B. Context and attribution

**Compliant**

> On **[Release]**, **[Contributor]** is credited for **[verified role]**. Listen for **[specific editorial observation]** in **[Track]**; it changes the shape of the ensemble’s **[plain-language musical detail]**. **[approved link]**

Why it works: the credit is verifiable, the listening note is clearly an editorial invitation, and the contributor is not erased.

**Non-compliant**

> **[Lead artist]** does everything on this record, proving no one else in the scene can touch them.

Why it fails: it collapses a collaboration into one person, makes a broad unsupported comparison, and invites antagonism.

### C. Subjective listening note

**Compliant**

> I keep returning to the way **[Artist]** lets **[instrument/ensemble]** hold the silence on **[Track]**. To my ear, that restraint makes the final entrance feel earned. **[approved link]**

Why it works: it names the work, makes clear that the interpretation is personal, and points to something a listener can hear.

**Non-compliant**

> **[Artist]** intentionally uses silence here to represent **[unverified personal or cultural claim]**.

Why it fails: it asserts artistic intent without evidence and turns interpretation into fact.

### D. Scene and identity

**Compliant**

> **[Artist]** recorded **[Work]** in **[verified place/context]** with **[verified collaborators]**. The result is not a genre lesson; start with **[specific track]** and follow the conversation between **[musical elements]**. **[approved link]**

Why it works: it uses documented context, names collaborators, and lets the music—not a stereotype—carry the recommendation.

**Non-compliant**

> You can hear the raw spirit of **[country/region]** in every note—this is what authentic jazz sounds like.

Why it fails: it exoticizes a place, essentializes identity, and makes an exclusionary authenticity claim.

## 8. Pre-publication checklist

Before submitting a revision for approval, the editor or editorial agent must confirm:

- [ ] One primary content pillar and one supported format are clear.
- [ ] Artist, work title, contributors, and release/performance details are correctly identified.
- [ ] Every factual claim has retained evidence; subjective language is plainly framed as opinion.
- [ ] The copy contains a concrete listening angle and no banned pattern.
- [ ] Links are approved, functional, and accurately labelled.
- [ ] Media provenance, permissions, credit, and alt text are complete when media is used.
- [ ] The draft satisfies current X formatting constraints and contains no unnecessary emojis or calls for engagement; hashtags only in `hashtags.json`, within the per-platform limits, and all relevant to the post (owner decision, 2026-10-16).
- [ ] The revision and its sources are ready for human review under the product approval workflow.

## 9. Governance and exceptions

These guidelines are a QA input, not a substitute for editorial judgment. A reviewer may approve an intentional exception only when the review record states the reason, identifies any added risk, and records the approving editor. Exceptions do not silently establish a new rule. Changes to these guidelines require a documented review and version-controlled update.
